# What the research says

Your photograph is a particularly good example of a broad "prospect over a patterned lowland" view.

The central finding is slightly paradoxical: **there is no scientifically accepted, observer-free scalar metric of scenic beauty**. What can be measured objectively is the visual and spatial character of the scene - its openness, depth, topography, visible land-cover pattern, atmospheric clarity, skyline and so on.

This is also how the landscape-science literature treats the problem. A widely cited framework identifies nine concepts - visual scale, complexity, coherence, naturalness, disturbance, stewardship, historicity, imageability and ephemera - but explicitly says these describe landscape character rather than form a normative visual-quality scale. A systematic review of 176 quantitative visibility studies found considerable variation in methods, tools and study designs, rather than one standard scenic-beauty metric. ([ResearchGate][1])

A useful mathematical distinction is:

[
\text{objective scene measurements} = m(S,v,t)
]

where (S) is the physical landscape, (v) is the viewpoint and viewing direction, and (t) is weather and lighting.

But:

[
\text{beauty judgement} = B(S,v,t,o)
]

also depends on an observer (o), including experience, attention and cultural context. Without observing (o), science can characterize the scene, but cannot establish that a particular combination is universally beautiful.

## What counts as genuinely objective

There are three different levels that are often confused:

1. **Feedback-free physical metrics**
   Elevation, visible solid angle, distance distribution, land-cover proportions, edge density, atmospheric visibility and similar quantities. These can be calculated without asking anyone whether they like the view.

2. **Objectively calculated but semantically interpreted metrics**
   "Naturalness", "disturbance", "stewardship", "coherence" and "historicity". The areas and objects can be measured objectively, but someone must decide that woodland is natural, a pylon is disturbing, or a field pattern is historically authentic.

3. **Human-feedback metrics**
   Scenic Beauty Estimation surveys, expert scores, social-media photo counts, likes, eye-tracking preference models and AI aesthetic models trained on ratings. These do not satisfy your constraint, even when the final calculation is automatic. Scenic Beauty Estimation, for example, is explicitly based on ratings of landscape photographs. ([ResearchGate][1])

I have therefore treated only the first category as strictly objective.

# The strongest objective metric families

## 1. Viewshed extent and openness

A **viewshed** identifies every surface visible from a viewpoint.

A basic measure is:

[
A_v=\sum_i V_i A_i
]

where (V_i) is 1 when terrain cell (i) is visible and 0 otherwise.

Useful associated quantities include:

* Total visible ground area
* Proportion of directions that are unobstructed
* Maximum, median and percentile horizon distances
* Sky View Factor
* Isovist area, volume, compactness and occlusivity
* Density of nearby obstructions
* Horizontal and vertical field of view

An isovist is the set of all points visible from a particular position. The original isovist work proposed numerical measures of its size and shape, allowing spaciousness and enclosure to be characterized geometrically. ([IDEAS/RePEc][2])

The weakness of an ordinary viewshed is that every square metre receives the same weight. A hectare 20 km away counts as much as a hectare immediately below the observer, even though their apparent sizes are radically different.

Another important issue is the elevation model. Bare-earth Digital Terrain Models omit trees and buildings and therefore generally overestimate what is actually visible. A review found that bare-earth models still dominated the field despite this limitation, so a Digital Surface Model containing vegetation and buildings is preferable for scenic-view analysis. ([USFS Research & Development][3])

## 2. Visual magnitude and angular occupancy

**Visual magnitude** improves on the binary viewshed by measuring how large a visible surface appears to the observer.

For a small visible surface element:

[
d\Omega_i \approx
\frac{A_i |\cos \theta_i|}{r_i^2}
]

where:

* (A_i) is the physical surface area
* (r_i) is its distance
* (\theta_i) is the angle between the surface and the viewing direction
* (d\Omega_i) is its approximate solid angle

This means nearby, face-on surfaces receive more weight than distant or sharply angled surfaces. Visual-magnitude research explicitly accounts for slope, aspect and distance relative to the observer. ([USU Institutional Repository][4])

For views, this is usually more meaningful than map area. For example, the relevant measure for woodland is not simply "how many hectares of woodland are inside the viewshed", but:

[
p_{\mathrm{woodland}} =
\frac{\sum_{i \in \mathrm{woodland}}\Delta\Omega_i}
{\sum_{i \in \mathrm{visible}}\Delta\Omega_i}
]

This produces the proportion of the observer's visual field occupied by woodland.

The same calculation can be made for fields, villages, roads, water, sky, electricity pylons and other features.

## 3. Depth and distance structure

A beautiful panoramic view often contains not only a distant horizon but a distribution of near, middle and far elements.

Established measurements include:

* Maximum visible distance, (D_{\max})
* Median visible distance, (D_{50})
* 90th or 95th percentile visible distance
* Standard deviation of visible distances, (\sigma_D)
* Proportion of visible solid angle in different distance bands
* Number of distinct occlusion or depth layers

The current Viewscape software defines depth as the furthest visible distance and depth variation as the standard deviation of distances to visible points. It also calculates extent, horizontal visible area, visible relief, Sky View Factor and skyline variation. ([CRAN][5])

A useful additional feedback-free metric would be **depth entropy**:

[
H_D=-\sum_b q_b \ln q_b
]

where (q_b) is the angular proportion of the scene in distance band (b). A scene in which nearly everything is at one distance has low depth entropy. A scene with substantial foreground, middle ground, distant ground and horizon has higher depth entropy.

This is better treated as a descriptor rather than as a rule that higher is always better.

## 4. Relative elevation and topographic dominance

Absolute elevation alone is a poor predictor of the view. A 500 m summit surrounded by 900 m ridges may have little prospect, while a 260 m escarpment above a flat plain can have an enormous one.

A better metric is the **Topographic Position Index**:

[
TPI_R=z_0-\overline{z}_R
]

where (z_0) is viewpoint elevation and (\overline{z}_R) is mean elevation within a selected surrounding radius (R).

Positive values indicate that the viewpoint stands above its surroundings. Calculating it at several radii distinguishes a small mound from a regionally dominant ridge or escarpment. ([ArcGIS Documentation][6])

Other objective terrain metrics include:

* Local prominence
* Slope beneath the viewpoint
* Terrain Ruggedness Index
* Visible elevation range
* Standard deviation of visible elevations
* Horizon-angle variation
* Skyline roughness and curvature
* Number and prominence of visible landform layers

Terrain Ruggedness Index measures local topographic heterogeneity from elevation differences between neighbouring cells. ([MSU Library][7])

Importantly, ruggedness describes one kind of landscape drama. It is not required for a strong view. Coombe Hill demonstrates how high relative dominance over a comparatively level vale can create great visual depth without mountain-scale relief.

## 5. Visible land-cover composition

Once the visible scene has been ray-traced, each visible surface can be assigned a class:

* Woodland
* Scrub
* Grassland
* Arable field
* Water
* Settlement
* Industrial land
* Roads
* Railways
* Electricity infrastructure
* Sky

The objective output should be a vector of angular proportions rather than a single number.

Landscape-character research commonly proposes open-land proportion, visible water, natural-vegetation proportion, visible landmarks and visible disturbance as indicators. It also proposes viewshed size, depth and obstruction density as measurements of visual scale. ([ResearchGate][8])

There is an important qualification: the raw proportion of woodland is objective, but calling it "natural" is an interpretation. The raw angular proportion of pylons is objective, but calling them "disturbance" is an interpretation that may vary by landscape and context.

## 6. Diversity and complexity

The patchwork character of a landscape can be measured using established landscape-ecology metrics.

### Shannon diversity

[
H=-\sum_k p_k\ln p_k
]

where (p_k) is the proportion of the visible scene belonging to class (k).

### Simpson diversity

[
S=1-\sum_k p_k^2
]

This has an intuitive interpretation: the probability that two randomly selected visible samples belong to different classes.

### Edge density

[
ED=\frac{L_{\mathrm{edges}}}{A_{\mathrm{visible}}}
]

or, for an observer-based version, boundary length can be measured in angular image coordinates.

### Other useful metrics

* Patch richness
* Patch density
* Mean patch size
* Patch-size variation
* Shape complexity
* Aggregation index
* Interspersion and juxtaposition
* Largest-patch proportion
* Boundary contrast
* Fractal dimension of boundaries or skyline

FRAGSTATS distinguishes composition, such as diversity and land-cover proportions, from configuration, such as edges, patch sizes and aggregation. It also warns that Shannon diversity has little absolute meaning, can ignore the identity of the classes, and should normally be used comparatively. Edge metrics are highly sensitive to spatial resolution, so two viewpoints should not be compared using data at different resolutions. ([Fragstats][9])

This leads to one of the most important findings: **complexity should not be maximized blindly**.

* Very low diversity can produce monotony.
* Very high edge density can produce visual clutter.
* Large homogeneous patches can be simple but powerful.
* A mixture of patch types can be varied yet coherent.
* Random fragmentation is different from an ordered agricultural mosaic.

Landscape-indicator research explicitly cautions that indicator relationships need not be linear and that perceptible changes may appear only after thresholds are crossed. ([ResearchGate][8])

For Coombe Hill, the most useful combination is therefore not "maximum entropy", but something closer to:

[
\text{moderate-high diversity}
+
\text{moderate-high aggregation and repetition}
]

That combination can be described as **ordered complexity**.

## 7. Coherence, stewardship, disturbance and historicity

These are important concepts but are only partly feedback-free.

Objective proxies for coherence include:

* Repetition of patch sizes and shapes
* Spatial autocorrelation
* Alignment of field boundaries
* Edge-orientation entropy
* Consistency of colours and textures
* Aggregation of related land-cover classes
* Continuity of hedgerows, woodland and watercourses

Objective proxies for stewardship include:

* Proportion of maintained versus abandoned land
* Vegetation condition
* Density of derelict structures
* Litter or debris coverage
* Regularity and continuity of maintained boundaries

Objective proxies for historicity include:

* Persistence of field boundaries across historical maps
* Age of woodland
* Density of listed or historic structures
* Proportion of traditional land uses
* Continuity of land-cover pattern through time

The landscape-character framework describes coherence as unity and repeating patterns of colour and texture, stewardship as visible order and care, and disturbance as lack of contextual fit. But it also acknowledges that choosing which elements count as disturbing or traditional depends on local interpretation. ([ResearchGate][8])

This is especially relevant to the English countryside. Coombe Hill's view is not wilderness. It is a managed landscape containing farms, villages, roads, pylons and cultivated fields. Its visual strength appears to involve **coherence and visible care as much as naturalness**.

## 8. Atmospheric clarity and atmospheric perspective

The atmosphere changes a view even when the physical landscape remains identical.

A standard objective measure is **Meteorological Optical Range**, or MOR. The Met Office defines this as the distance over which a light beam is reduced to 5 percent of its original intensity. ([Met Office][10])

Using the Beer-Lambert law:

[
T(d)=e^{-\beta d}
]

and:

[
MOR=\frac{-\ln(0.05)}{\beta}
\approx \frac{3}{\beta}
]

where (\beta) is the atmospheric extinction coefficient.

Other objective atmospheric and illumination metrics include:

* Aerosol optical depth
* Cloud fraction
* Solar elevation and azimuth
* Direct-to-diffuse light ratio
* Shadow fraction
* Scene luminance range
* Contrast attenuation with distance
* Saturation attenuation with distance
* Colour-temperature gradient
* Visibility of the most distant known landmark

Atmospheric perspective is a physical effect in which distant objects become lighter, less saturated and less distinct as light is scattered and absorbed. ([ResearchGate][11])

But perfect clarity is not automatically superior. Very clear air reveals detail, while moderate haze can separate the scene into distinct depth layers. The objective quantities can be measured, but selecting an ideal amount of haze is again an aesthetic judgement.

## 9. Landmarks, uniqueness and ephemera

The literature calls a scene's ability to be distinctive and memorable **imageability**. Suggested indicators include the visible density of landmarks, iconic elements and unique features. It also uses **ephemera** to describe seasonal and weather-related change. ([ResearchGate][8])

Potential objective metrics include:

* Landmark angular size
* Local colour or luminance contrast
* Isolation from surrounding objects
* Number of visually unique objects
* Rarity relative to other views in the same region
* Seasonal colour variability
* Frequency of visible wildlife, balloons, cloud shadows or agricultural activity
* Probability of a particular weather-light combination

A fully feedback-free uniqueness score could use statistical rarity:

[
U=-\ln P(\mathbf{x}\mid \text{region, season})
]

where (\mathbf{x}) is the scene's metric vector.

This measures how unusual the view is relative to nearby views. It does not prove that unusual means beautiful, but it provides an objective component of distinctiveness.

## 10. Photograph-only metrics

A photograph can also be analysed using:

* Pixel entropy
* Colour entropy
* Edge density
* Edge-orientation entropy
* Symmetry
* Visual balance
* Spatial-frequency spectrum
* Fractal dimension
* Compression ratio
* Saliency concentration
* Foreground-to-background contrast

These are reproducible, but they are often poor place-level metrics because they depend heavily on:

* Lens and field of view
* Crop and camera direction
* Exposure and white balance
* Sharpening and noise reduction
* Time of day
* Photographer composition

Some apparently objective aesthetic formulas, such as preferred fractal-dimension ranges or colourfulness scores, were calibrated against human judgements. Under your strict definition, they should not be treated as feedback-free measures of beauty.

# The most defensible no-feedback measurement set

For a rigorous comparison of viewpoints, I would not calculate one beauty score. I would report a **view-character vector** containing at least:

[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{x} = (&TPI,\ A_v,\ \Omega_v,\ SVF,\ D_{50},\ D_{95},\ \sigma_D,\
&\sigma_z,\ \sigma_{\mathrm{skyline}},\ \mathbf{p}*{\mathrm{landcover}},\
&SHDI,\ SIDI,\ ED,\ AI,\ f*{\mathrm{built}},\ MOR)
\end{aligned}
]

In ordinary language:

| Dimension         | Core measurements                                                    |
| ----------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Relative position | TPI and local prominence at several radii                            |
| Openness          | Visible area, visible solid angle and Sky View Factor                |
| Distance          | Median, 95th percentile, maximum and variation                       |
| Landform          | Visible relief and skyline variation                                 |
| Composition       | Angular fractions of every land-cover and infrastructure class       |
| Diversity         | Shannon, Simpson and patch richness                                  |
| Configuration     | Edge density, aggregation and patch-size variation                   |
| Intrusion         | Angular fraction of buildings, roads, pylons and industrial features |
| Atmosphere        | MOR and distance-dependent contrast                                  |
| Uniqueness        | Statistical rarity relative to comparable regional viewpoints        |

Each value should be reported separately or as a regional percentile.

A weighted sum such as:

[
0.3,\text{openness}
+0.2,\text{diversity}
+0.2,\text{naturalness}
+\ldots
]

would no longer be fully objective. The choice of weights, signs and thresholds would encode someone's idea of beauty. Equal weights are also a human choice.

## Approximate measurements from your JPEG

I made a simple image-level analysis of the attached 1536 by 1152 JPEG. These are not full site measurements because the image does not contain camera heading, lens information, a depth map or a 360-degree panorama.

* The sky occupies approximately **15 percent** of the image height.
* Dividing the landscape into rough horizontal foreground, middle and distant bands, mean colour saturation in the near band is approximately **2.9 times** that in the distant band.
* Fine-detail energy in the near band is approximately **2.2 times** that in the distant band.
* The distant band is much lighter than the foreground.
* The balloon occupies less than approximately **0.05 percent** of the image, but its colour contrast and isolation make it disproportionately salient.

The near-to-far saturation, contrast and detail gradient is a strong objective signature of atmospheric perspective. The foreground foliage supplies scale and enclosure, while the receding fields supply depth. The balloon is a particularly clear example of an ephemeral focal element: physically tiny, but optically distinctive.

The view's principal ingredients therefore appear to be:

[
\boxed{
\text{high relative elevation}
+
\text{large visual depth}
+
\text{ordered landscape diversity}
+
\text{low angular obstruction}
+
\text{atmospheric layering}
+
\text{a salient focal element}
}
]

It is notable that rugged mountains, a dramatic coastline and a dominant body of water are not required. This landscape derives much of its character from an extensive, legible patchwork and from the contrast between a contained foreground and an exceptionally broad prospect.

# How a rigorous Coombe Hill study could be conducted

A defensible analysis would proceed as follows.

1. **Fix the observation geometry**
   Record exact coordinates, eye height, azimuth and field of view. Ideally use a calibrated 360-degree panorama so that the photographer's crop does not determine the result.

2. **Use both terrain and surface elevation**
   Use a Digital Terrain Model for landform and a Digital Surface Model for actual visibility through trees and buildings. England has Environment Agency 1 m DTM and DSM products covering approximately 99 percent of the country. ([Defra Data Services][13])

3. **Calculate the viewshed and visual magnitude**
   Ray-trace visible cells, including surface obstructions, Earth curvature for long distances and a stated atmospheric-refraction assumption. Convert visible cells to solid-angle weights rather than simply counting square metres.

4. **Overlay land cover and infrastructure**
   Natural England's Living England project provides a national satellite-derived habitat layer that can supply broad habitat classes. Buildings, roads, railway lines and power infrastructure can be added from mapping datasets. ([Data.gov.uk][14])

5. **Calculate geometry and landscape metrics**
   The current Viewscape R package, version 3.0.0 published in June 2026, calculates viewsheds, visual magnitude, extent, depth, relief, Sky View Factor, skyline variation, patch metrics, Shannon diversity and visible feature proportions. FRAGSTATS can provide additional patch-composition and configuration metrics. ([CRAN][15])

6. **Add atmospheric conditions**
   Use observed or modelled visibility, cloud, solar angle and aerosol information. The Met Office provides UK observation archives including MIDAS-Open, while MOR provides the physically relevant visibility measure. ([Met Office][16])

7. **Repeat over time**
   Calculate the view under leaf-on and leaf-off conditions, different crop seasons, several solar angles and a distribution of atmospheric visibility. A place has a distribution of views, not one permanent visual state.

8. **Compare only like with like**
   All viewpoints should use the same eye height, radius, DSM resolution, land-cover classification, atmosphere assumptions and viewing geometry. Edge density and patch metrics should never be compared across different raster resolutions.

9. **Report percentiles, not beauty points**
   For example: "Coombe Hill is in the 95th percentile for depth, the 90th percentile for relative topographic position, the 80th percentile for visible land-cover diversity and the 25th percentile for skyline ruggedness among Chiltern viewpoints."

That would be objective, reproducible and meaningful without claiming that a particular percentile combination is universally beautiful.

# Bottom line

The mature scientific answer is not an observer-free beauty score. It is a multidimensional measurement of the physical view.

For Coombe Hill, the strongest objectively measurable ingredients are likely:

* High elevation relative to the surrounding vale
* A large unobstructed angular field
* Long and varied visible distances
* Strong separation of foreground, middle ground and horizon
* A diverse but spatially coherent field-woodland mosaic
* Relatively low angular dominance of infrastructure
* Clear atmospheric perspective
* Seasonal and transient focal elements, in this case the balloon

Those properties explain **how the visual scene is structured**. Calling that particular structure beautiful remains the final human judgement, but the ingredients themselves can be quantified without surveys, ratings, likes or aesthetic-model training.
