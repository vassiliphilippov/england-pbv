The scientific problem is called **terrain viewshed analysis**. Given an observer position and a digital elevation model, or DEM, it determines which parts of the terrain have an unobstructed line of sight to the observer.

Your theory is broadly correct, with one important refinement:

> What matters is not absolute altitude, but **angular dominance over the surrounding terrain**.

Adding 500 metres to every elevation, including the viewpoint, changes nothing. A viewpoint is good geometrically when the terrain falls away from it and remains below the observer's lines of sight over a large range of directions and distances. Viewshed science formalises exactly this idea. ([arXiv][1])

# 1. The fundamental line-of-sight calculation

Suppose the terrain is represented by a height function

[
z=f(x,y)
]

where (x,y) are horizontal coordinates and (z) is elevation.

The observer is at

[
O=(x_0,y_0,z_O)
]

with

[
z_O=f(x_0,y_0)+h_O
]

where (h_O) is the observer's eye height, usually around 1.6-1.8 metres.

A target terrain point is

[
P=(x,y,z_P)
]

where

[
z_P=f(x,y)+h_T
]

and (h_T) is the target height:

* (h_T=0) if you are asking whether the ground itself is visible
* (h_T=1.7) metres if you are asking whether another standing person would be visible
* (h_T=20) metres if the target is the top of a building or tree

The horizontal distance is

[
d=\sqrt{(x-x_0)^2+(y-y_0)^2}
]

The straight line from observer to target is

[
L(s)=O+s(P-O),\qquad 0\leq s\leq1
]

The target is visible exactly when the line remains above the terrain at every intermediate point:

[
L_z(s)>f\left(x_0+s(x-x_0),\ y_0+s(y-y_0)\right)
]

for every (0<s<1).

That is the mathematically fundamental definition. All practical viewshed algorithms are faster ways of evaluating this condition across millions of target cells. ([arXiv][1])

## The elevation-angle formulation

The same calculation becomes much more intuitive when expressed as angles.

For a terrain point at distance (r) and bearing (\theta), define its elevation angle from the observer as

[
\alpha(r,\theta)=
\operatorname{atan2}\left(
f(r,\theta)-z_O,\ r
\right)
]

For a candidate point at distance (d), calculate the largest elevation angle of all terrain before it:

[
H_{<d}(\theta)=
\max_{0<r<d}\alpha(r,\theta)
]

The candidate is visible when

[
\alpha(d,\theta)\geq H_{<d}(\theta)
]

apart from a small numerical tolerance.

In plain English:

> A point is visible when it establishes a new elevation-angle record along its direction from the observer.

Consider a radial elevation profile:

```text
observer       near hill       hidden valley       distant mountain
   O              /\                 __                  /\
   |             /  \_______________/  \________________/  \
```

The near hill may block the valley, but the distant mountain can become visible again if its elevation angle rises above the near hill. Consequently, visible terrain along one direction is not necessarily one continuous interval.

This also explains your steep-decline intuition. When terrain immediately falls away, the initial elevation angles are strongly negative. A distant field, hill or town therefore has a greater chance of rising above the previous angular maximum.

A nearby obstacle is especially damaging because angle depends on height divided by distance. An obstacle whose top is 2 metres above the eye at 10 metres has an angle of

[
\arctan(2/10)=11.3^\circ
]

whereas a hill 20 metres above the eye at 1 kilometre has an angle of only

[
\arctan(20/1000)=1.15^\circ
]

This is why a modest hedge or line of trees near a viewpoint can block more of the panorama than a substantial distant hill.

# 2. Earth curvature and atmospheric refraction

For short distances, a flat-Earth calculation is normally sufficient. Over tens of kilometres, the curvature of the Earth becomes significant.

In a local tangent coordinate system, terrain at distance (r) can be curvature-corrected as

[
z_c(r)=z(r)-\frac{(1-k)r^2}{2R_E}
]

where:

* (R_E) is the Earth's radius, approximately (6.371\times10^6) metres
* (k) is the atmospheric refraction coefficient
* (k=0) means straight light rays with no refraction
* (k\approx1/7) is a conventional standard optical-refraction assumption

The elevation-angle calculation then becomes

[
\alpha_c(r,\theta)=
\operatorname{atan2}
\left(
z_c(r,\theta)-z_O,\ r
\right)
]

GDAL expresses the same correction using

[
C=1-k
]

and subtracts

[
\frac{Cr^2}{2R_E}
]

from the terrain elevation. Its conventional visible-light setting uses (k=1/7), giving (C=6/7). ([GDAL][2])

With standard (k=1/7), the effective curvature drop is approximately:

| Distance | Curvature correction |
| -------: | -------------------: |
|     5 km |                1.7 m |
|    10 km |                6.7 m |
|    20 km |               26.9 m |
|    40 km |              107.6 m |

So curvature has little importance for nearby trees and fields, but it can materially change whether distant hills are visible.

An equivalent formulation uses an effective Earth radius:

[
R_{\text{eff}}=\frac{R_E}{1-k}
]

For (k=1/7),

[
R_{\text{eff}}=\frac{7}{6}R_E
]

For a smooth Earth, the approximate distance to the horizon from height (h) above the reference surface is

[
d_h\approx\sqrt{2R_{\text{eff}}h}
]

For two elevated points of heights (h_1) and (h_2), their approximate maximum mutual line-of-sight distance is

[
d_{\max}\approx
\sqrt{2R_{\text{eff}}h_1}+
\sqrt{2R_{\text{eff}}h_2}
]

Atmospheric refraction varies with temperature and pressure gradients. A reproducible system should therefore report its assumed (k), and ideally calculate results for at least (k=0) and (k=1/7). Refraction changes geometric ray paths; haze and mist are a separate phenomenon affecting whether geometrically visible terrain can actually be distinguished.

For very long ranges, a more exact method is to transform the observer and terrain mesh into Earth-centred Cartesian coordinates and perform line-triangle intersection against an ellipsoidal Earth model. The quadratic correction is usually adequate for ordinary regional viewsheds.

# 3. "Area of view" has several different meanings

There is no single uniquely correct definition. At least four quantities are scientifically defensible, and they measure different things.

## 3.1 Planimetric visible area

This is the most common GIS definition:

[
A_{\text{plan}}=
\iint_D V(x,y),dx,dy
]

where

[
V(x,y)=
\begin{cases}
1,&\text{visible}\
0,&\text{hidden}
\end{cases}
]

For raster cells:

[
A_{\text{plan}}\approx\sum_i V_i a_i
]

where (a_i) is the horizontal map area of cell (i).

For a one-metre raster, a fully visible cell normally contributes approximately one square metre. For a five-metre raster, it contributes approximately 25 square metres.

In polar coordinates:

[
A_{\text{plan}}=
\int_0^{2\pi}
\int_0^{R_{\max}}
V(r,\theta),r,dr,d\theta
]

You may sometimes see the simpler formula

[
A=\frac12\int_0^{2\pi}r_*(\theta)^2,d\theta
]

but this is valid only when everything from the observer to one final boundary (r_*(\theta)) is visible. Real terrain normally violates this condition because a nearby hill can hide a valley while a more distant hill becomes visible again.

Planimetric area answers:

> How many hectares of horizontal land surface can theoretically be seen?

It does not answer how visually large those hectares appear.

## 3.2 Actual terrain surface area

A sloping hillside has more physical surface area than its footprint on a horizontal map.

For a differentiable terrain surface,

[
A_{\text{surface}}=
\iint_D
V(x,y)
\sqrt{1+f_x^2+f_y^2}
,dx,dy
]

In practice, the DEM is converted into triangles and the visible triangle areas are summed.

This metric can be useful for environmental exposure calculations, but it is not necessarily better for scenic views. It rewards steepness and roughness, and it changes substantially with DEM resolution because finer data represent more small-scale surface variation.

## 3.3 Angular area or solid angle

Human vision is angular. A large distant region can occupy less of the visual field than a small nearby cliff.

For a visible surface element (dA), its approximate solid angle is

[
d\Omega=
V,
\frac{\max(0,\hat n\cdot\hat s)}
{r^2}
,dA
]

where:

* (r) is distance to the surface element
* (\hat n) is its surface normal
* (\hat s) points from the surface towards the observer
* (\Omega) is measured in steradians

This formula automatically gives less visual importance to distant terrain and more importance to surfaces facing the observer. Distance, solid angle and surface orientation are established geometric components of visual-exposure analysis. ([Addi EHU][3])

For a fully visible triangular surface patch, an exact solid angle can be calculated. Let (\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c) be vectors from the observer to the triangle's vertices:

[
\Omega_\triangle=
2\operatorname{atan2}
\left(
|\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)|,\
|\mathbf a||\mathbf b||\mathbf c|
+(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)|\mathbf c|
+(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf c)|\mathbf a|
+(\mathbf c\cdot\mathbf a)|\mathbf b|
\right)
]

Summing this over visible triangles gives the apparent angular size of the visible terrain.

A computationally simpler approximation is to render a 360-degree panorama or spherical depth map from the viewpoint and count the angular pixels occupied by visible terrain.

For scenic-view ranking, angular area is often more meaningful than raw hectares. It should nevertheless exclude or separately measure the first 20-100 metres, because the ground immediately surrounding the observer otherwise occupies a very large angular area.

## 3.4 Horizon profile and openness

For every horizontal bearing (\theta), define the final terrain horizon angle

[
H(\theta)=
\max_{0<r<R_{\max}}
\alpha_c(r,\theta)
]

and the distance at which it occurs:

[
r_H(\theta)=
\operatorname*{argmax}*{0<r<R*{\max}}
\alpha_c(r,\theta)
]

The result is a 360-degree horizon profile. GIS horizon algorithms trace each bearing and retain the maximum terrain angle, including optional Earth-curvature corrections. ([GRASS][4])

Useful horizon metrics include:

* Mean and median horizon elevation
* 90th-percentile horizon elevation
* Mean and median horizon distance
* Fraction of bearings with horizon below (0^\circ), (2^\circ), (5^\circ), and so on
* Width of the largest continuous open sector
* Separate best 90-degree, 120-degree and 180-degree sectors

This captures the difference between:

* an isolated summit with a 360-degree panorama
* an escarpment such as Coombe Hill with an exceptional view predominantly on one side
* a high point surrounded by slightly higher terrain

A related quantity is the **sky-view factor**. For a horizontal receiving surface and the conventional upper-sky hemisphere, it is approximately

[
SVF=
\frac{1}{2\pi}
\int_0^{2\pi}
\cos^2\left(H_+(\theta)\right)
,d\theta
]

where

[
H_+(\theta)=\max(0,H(\theta))
]

Sky-view factor is primarily a radiative quantity, measuring how much diffuse sky radiation reaches a surface under particular assumptions. It is related to openness, but it is not identical to the visually perceived fraction of sky or to scenic quality. Different disciplines also use somewhat different sky-view-factor definitions. ([GMD][5])

The horizon profile alone is not sufficient to reconstruct visible land area. It records the highest angular terrain in each direction, but not all the nearer terrain that established earlier angular records.

# 4. How viewshed algorithms calculate visibility

Assume there are (n) raster cells in the analysis region.

## 4.1 Independent ray casting

The conceptually simplest method is:

1. Select one target cell.
2. Draw a ray from the observer to the target.
3. Sample or intersect the terrain along that ray.
4. Find the maximum intervening elevation angle.
5. Mark the target visible or hidden.
6. Repeat for every cell.

For a square raster with side length (m), there are (n=m^2) target cells and an average ray crosses (O(m)) cells. Total work is therefore approximately

[
O(m^3)=O(n^{3/2})
]

This family includes the commonly discussed R3 reference algorithm. It is relatively direct and accurate under the chosen terrain interpolation model, but expensive for large rasters. ([arXiv][1])

## 4.2 Rotational sweep-line algorithms

A faster method rotates a ray around the observer.

Each raster cell has:

* an angular entry event
* a central event
* an angular exit event

As the ray rotates, the algorithm maintains the cells intersected by the current ray, ordered by distance. At each target event it compares the target's elevation angle against the maximum angle among nearer active cells.

This can calculate a viewshed in approximately

[
O(n\log n)
]

time. GRASS GIS `r.viewshed` uses a radial sweep approach and models intermediate terrain using bilinear interpolation between neighbouring raster elevations. ([GRASS][6])

## 4.3 Centrifugal and horizon-propagation algorithms

Another family processes cells outward from the observer. Each newly processed cell inherits or interpolates information about the current horizon from nearer cells.

These methods can approach

[
O(n)
]

time, but their accuracy depends on:

* how directions are discretised
* how the horizon is interpolated
* the order in which cells are processed
* assumptions about the terrain between cell centres

They are useful for very large calculations, but should be validated against a slower reference method.

## 4.4 Reference-plane methods

Reference-plane algorithms compare new terrain cells against visibility planes formed by previously processed cells, avoiding a complete ray trace to every target. GDAL's `gdal_viewshed` implementation is based on the Wang 2000 method. GDAL can produce a binary viewshed, a minimum visible target-height raster, or cumulative visibility results. ([GDAL][2])

## 4.5 Triangulated-terrain ray tracing

Instead of treating the DEM as raster cells, each cell can be divided into triangles, producing a continuous piecewise-planar terrain mesh.

Visibility then becomes a conventional 3D ray-triangle intersection problem:

* cast a segment from the observer to the target
* find the first terrain intersection
* the target is visible only if no earlier intersection occurs

A triangulated irregular network can represent ridgelines, breaklines and cliffs more faithfully than a regular raster, provided those features are present in the source data.

## Simplified conceptual algorithm

A basic angular implementation looks like this:

```text
for each bearing theta:
    maximum_angle = -infinity

    for terrain samples in increasing distance r:
        z = interpolate_terrain(theta, r)
        z = z - (1 - k) * r^2 / (2 * earth_radius)

        angle = atan2(z - observer_eye_elevation, r)

        if angle >= maximum_angle - tolerance:
            mark sample visible
            maximum_angle = max(maximum_angle, angle)
        else:
            mark sample hidden
```

This demonstrates the mathematics, but a production raster implementation must handle rays crossing cells, angular gaps, interpolation and partial-cell visibility. Simply tracing a modest number of equally spaced rays can miss narrow obstacles between rays.

# 5. The elevation map is not the terrain itself

A raster contains elevation samples, not a complete mathematical surface. You have to decide what exists between them.

Common models are:

1. **Piecewise constant cells** - every cell is a flat block.
2. **Bilinear interpolation** - elevation changes smoothly between four neighbouring samples.
3. **Triangulated interpolation** - each raster square becomes two planar triangles.
4. **TIN with breaklines** - ridges, cliffs and streams can be explicitly preserved.

Different choices can produce different viewsheds, especially near narrow ridges and grazing lines of sight. This model-selection issue is central in the scientific literature on raster viewsheds. ([arXiv][1])

You must also define what it means for a raster cell to be visible:

* its centre is visible
* at least one corner is visible
* every corner is visible
* some estimated fraction of its area is visible

For area measurement, the last definition is best. A practical approximation is to subdivide boundary cells into smaller samples and estimate the visible fraction:

[
A_{\text{plan}}\approx\sum_i p_i a_i
]

where (p_i\in[0,1]) is the visible fraction of cell (i).

# 6. Bare-earth terrain versus trees and buildings

A map of ground altitude alone cannot measure whether trees are blocking the view.

Two different elevation products are required:

* **DTM**, digital terrain model - approximately bare-earth elevation, with vegetation and buildings removed
* **DSM**, digital surface model - elevations of the upper surface, including tree canopies, buildings and other objects

Features smaller than the raster cell cannot be represented explicitly, and DSMs and DTMs describe fundamentally different surfaces. ([USGS][7])

For your project, calculate at least three related viewsheds.

## Terrain-potential viewshed

Use the DTM both for targets and blockers:

[
V_{\text{terrain}}=
\operatorname{Viewshed}(\text{DTM})
]

This answers:

> How large would the view be if all vegetation and buildings were removed?

## Current visible-surface viewshed

Use the DSM as both target and blocker:

[
V_{\text{surface}}=
\operatorname{Viewshed}(\text{DSM})
]

This determines which canopy tops, roofs and other surface elements are visible.

## DSM-blocked ground viewshed

Use DTM elevations as targets, but the DSM as the blocking surface:

[
V_{\text{ground through obstacles}}=
\operatorname{ViewshedTargets}
(\text{DTM},\text{blockers}=\text{DSM})
]

This is the most direct answer to:

> How much actual landscape ground is visible given today's trees and buildings?

The obstruction loss can then be measured as

[
L_{\text{obstruction}}=
A(V_{\text{terrain}})
---------------------

A(V_{\text{ground through obstacles}})
]

A normal DSM treats a tree canopy as opaque. Real woodland has gaps, seasonal leaf changes and partial transmission. More accurate modelling would use the original LiDAR point cloud or a voxelised canopy model, assigning a probability of transmission along each ray. A leaf-on and leaf-off calculation would also be useful.

For England, the Environment Agency currently provides both 1-metre DTM and 1-metre DSM composites covering about 99 percent of the country, making this two-surface calculation unusually feasible at national scale. ([Defra Data Services][8])

# 7. Resolution matters enormously

A viewshed based on a 25-metre DEM will not reliably represent:

* hedges
* individual tree lines
* narrow ridges
* road embankments
* small buildings
* walls
* the exact edge of an escarpment

A feature smaller than the raster-cell dimensions may disappear entirely or be averaged into the surrounding terrain. ([USGS][7])

At the same time, running every national calculation at one-metre resolution would be computationally expensive. A good multiscale design is:

* 1-metre DSM and DTM for approximately the first 1-3 km
* 2-5 metre data for the intermediate landscape
* 10-25 metre terrain data for distant hills
* Earth curvature for the complete calculation

Nearby resolution matters disproportionately because small nearby obstacles occupy large elevation angles.

Resolution should be tested experimentally. For each viewpoint, calculate results at several resolutions and compare:

[
A_{1m},\quad A_{2m},\quad A_{5m},\quad A_{10m}
]

A stable viewpoint will retain a high score across resolutions. A viewpoint whose score collapses when detailed obstacles are included is less robust.

# 8. Continuous visibility is better than a binary answer

A binary visible-hidden map conceals how close each target is to being blocked.

Let

[
G_i=\max_{r<d_i}\alpha_c(r,\theta_i)
]

be the maximum blocker angle before target (i), and let (\alpha_i) be the target's angle.

Define the angular visibility margin:

[
m_i=\alpha_i-G_i
]

Then:

* (m_i>0) means visible
* (m_i<0) means hidden
* (m_i\approx0) means highly uncertain or grazing the horizon

The minimum extra target height required for visibility is

[
h_{\min,i}=
\max\left(
0,,
z_O+d_i\tan G_i-z_{c,i}
\right)
]

This answers questions such as:

* Is the ground visible?
* Would a two-metre person be visible?
* Would the top of a 20-metre tree be visible?
* How much would an elevation error change the answer?

GDAL supports output modes based on the minimum target height required for visibility, rather than only a binary raster. ([GDAL][2])

# 9. Error and probabilistic viewsheds

Binary viewsheds often look more certain than the source data justify. Visibility near a horizon can change because of:

* vertical DEM error
* horizontal location error
* interpolation model
* cell resolution
* observer-position error
* observer-height assumptions
* unknown canopy shape
* atmospheric refraction
* missing data
* clipping at the edge of the analysis area

A scientifically stronger approach is a **probabilistic viewshed**.

Generate (K) plausible terrain realisations:

[
f^{(k)}(x,y)=f(x,y)+e^{(k)}(x,y)
]

where (e^{(k)}) is a simulated, spatially correlated DEM error field. Calculate a viewshed for each realisation.

The estimated probability that cell (i) is visible is

[
p_i=\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^K V_i^{(k)}
]

Then the expected visible area is

[
E[A]=\sum_i p_i a_i
]

You can additionally report:

[
A_{\text{robust}}=
\sum_{i:p_i\geq0.9}a_i
]

and the uncertain boundary area:

[
A_{\text{uncertain}}=
\sum_{i:0.1<p_i<0.9}a_i
]

Research on viewshed uncertainty uses repeated plausible DEM simulations precisely because deterministic binary viewsheds can overstate certainty. Errors should normally be spatially correlated rather than independent from pixel to pixel. ([CAA Online Proceedings][9])

# 10. Raw visible area is not sufficient for ranking viewpoints

Raw area strongly favours distant land because annulus area grows with distance.

For example, the area between 20 and 40 km is

[
\pi(40^2-20^2)=1200\pi
]

while the entire area inside 20 km is only

[
\pi20^2=400\pi
]

So the 20-40 km ring contains three times as much land as everything inside 20 km. A viewpoint may obtain a huge raw score by seeing small fragments of flat distant terrain, even though those fragments are barely perceptible.

A better output is visible area by distance band:

[
A_j=
\sum_i
V_i a_i
\mathbf{1}
{r_j\leq d_i<r_{j+1}}
]

For example:

| Band     | Interpretation                  |
| -------- | ------------------------------- |
| 0-1 km   | Immediate setting               |
| 1-5 km   | Nearby landscape                |
| 5-10 km  | Middle-distance landscape       |
| 10-20 km | Long view                       |
| 20-40 km | Distant horizon                 |
| 40-60 km | Exceptional-distance visibility |

A general distance-weighted metric is

[
A_w=\sum_i V_i a_i w(d_i)
]

but the choice of (w(d)) introduces an arbitrary preference. Reporting distance bands alongside angular area is more transparent.

# 11. Directional structure matters

Coombe Hill appears to illustrate an important case: the view need not be equally open in every direction. An escarpment viewpoint can have a spectacular 120-degree panorama while being blocked behind.

Divide the horizon into azimuth bins. For bin (j), calculate

[
S_j=
\sum_{i\in j}
V_i a_i w(d_i)
]

Then measure:

* Total 360-degree score
* Best continuous 90-degree sector
* Best continuous 120-degree sector
* Best continuous 180-degree sector
* Fraction of azimuths exceeding a minimum openness threshold
* Largest uninterrupted open sector
* Distribution of visible area among directions

This distinguishes a broad coherent panorama from many small disconnected gaps through trees.

You can also calculate directional entropy. Let

[
p_j=\frac{S_j}{\sum_k S_k}
]

Then

[
E_{\text{azimuth}}=
-\frac{\sum_j p_j\log p_j}{\log N}
]

where (N) is the number of azimuth bins.

* High entropy means visibility is distributed around the observer.
* Low entropy means visibility is concentrated in one or two directions.

Neither is inherently more beautiful, so both the total and best-sector values should be retained.

# 12. Topographic openness as a fast prefilter

A full viewshed for every square metre of England would be unnecessarily expensive. A faster first-stage metric is **topographic openness**.

For each candidate location, sample several directions and measure the angle between the local surface and surrounding horizon within a fixed radius. High positive openness generally identifies:

* exposed summits
* ridge crests
* escarpment edges
* convex hills
* isolated high ground

Low openness identifies enclosed valleys, hollows and locations surrounded by higher terrain.

Topographic openness is closely related to your theory and is useful for candidate generation, but it is not the same as visible area. It compresses each direction into one horizon angle and does not retain the complete pattern of visible and hidden terrain. ([Relief Visualization Toolbox][10])

A national search can therefore use:

1. Topographic openness, local convexity and relative elevation to find candidate points.
2. Land-cover and canopy-height filters to remove woodland interiors.
3. Access and path data to retain reachable locations.
4. Full high-resolution viewsheds for the remaining candidates.
5. Angular, directional and uncertainty metrics for final ranking.

# 13. The objective metric set I would use

I would not reduce everything immediately to one "beauty score". I would store the following objective geometric feature vector for every candidate viewpoint:

[
M(p)=
\left[
A_{\text{bands}},
\Omega_{\text{terrain}},
C_{\text{azimuth}},
B_{90},
B_{120},
H_{\text{median}},
H_{90},
D_{\text{horizon}},
R_{\text{visible}},
L_{\text{obstruction}},
A_{\text{robust}}
\right]
]

where:

| Feature                    | Meaning                                         |
| -------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| (A_{\text{bands}})         | Visible plan area in distance bands             |
| (\Omega_{\text{terrain}})  | Apparent angular size of visible terrain        |
| (C_{\text{azimuth}})       | Fraction of directions with meaningful openness |
| (B_{90},B_{120})           | Best 90-degree and 120-degree panorama scores   |
| (H_{\text{median}},H_{90}) | Typical and high horizon elevation angles       |
| (D_{\text{horizon}})       | Distribution of horizon distances               |
| (R_{\text{visible}})       | Visible elevation relief and variation          |
| (L_{\text{obstruction}})   | View lost to trees and buildings                |
| (A_{\text{robust}})        | Area visible despite plausible data errors      |

Visible relief could include:

[
R_{\text{visible}}=
Q_{95}(z_{\text{visible}})
--------------------------

Q_5(z_{\text{visible}})
]

as well as the range of visible elevation angles. This separates a view across varied valleys and ridges from an equally large but nearly flat plain.

A reproducible initial specification for England could be:

* Observer eye height: 1.7 m
* Fixed maximum radius: 40 km
* Separate DTM and DSM calculations
* Curvature results for (k=0) and (k=1/7)
* Visible area in 0-1, 1-5, 5-10, 10-20 and 20-40 km bands
* Best 90-degree, 120-degree and 180-degree sectors
* Terrain solid angle excluding the nearest 50 m
* Visibility margin for every cell
* Robust area under DEM and refraction uncertainty
* Local one-metre data combined with progressively coarser distant terrain

# Central conclusion

The mathematically correct primitive is:

[
\boxed{
\text{target angle}
-------------------

\text{largest intervening terrain angle}
}
]

not elevation alone.

A geometrically exceptional viewpoint normally has:

* high elevation relative to its surroundings
* a convex summit, ridge or escarpment position
* a steep fall-away close to the observer
* low nearby obstruction angles
* wide open azimuth sectors
* distant terrain that remains visible or re-emerges above intermediate horizons
* little canopy or building obstruction
* stable visibility despite DEM and atmospheric uncertainty

That describes the geometry visible at places such as Coombe Hill much more accurately than simply saying that they are high. The most meaningful objective result is not one visible-area number, but a combination of plan area, angular area, horizon openness, directional continuity, distance structure and obstacle loss.
