Yes. England is unusually well suited to this project because the Environment Agency publishes both:

* a bare-earth terrain model, which tells you the shape of the land
* a surface model, which includes trees, buildings and other objects that can block the view

In GIS terminology, the calculation you need is a **viewshed analysis**.

Your theory is a very good starting point, but I would not use visible ground area as the only metric. A flat open plain might have an enormous visible area but produce a less dramatic view than an escarpment overlooking a valley. I would separately measure openness, relative relief, visible distance, nearby obstructions and the composition of the visible landscape.

## The most practical architecture

The important engineering decision is to separate **national discovery** from **high-resolution verification**.

### 1. Search the entire country at 10m resolution

Start with the EA 10m DTM. Generate potential viewpoints only where somebody could plausibly stand:

* along OSM footpaths and tracks
* inside or near CRoW access land
* at existing OSM viewpoints
* around local peaks, ridges and escarpment edges
* near car parks or roads, depending on the type of visitor you want to serve

Sampling every 25-50m along paths should be sufficient for the initial search. There is no reason to evaluate every 1m pixel of England.

For every candidate, perform a fast horizon calculation over the 10m terrain. Trace rays in perhaps 360 or 720 directions and record the maximum terrain angle encountered in each direction.

This produces a compact **horizon profile** for every point and is substantially cheaper than calculating a complete high-resolution viewshed everywhere.

### 2. Recalculate the best candidates at 1m resolution

For perhaps the best 1-5% of candidates, load a local window of:

* 1m DTM
* 1m first-return DSM
* Vegetation Object Model
* Living England habitat data
* paths and access polygons

Calculate the observer's eye position from the DTM:

```text
observer height = ground elevation + approximately 1.7m
```

Do not place the observer on the DSM because that can accidentally put the observer on top of a tree or building.

For obstruction cells, use the first-return DSM, or construct a cleaner surface from:

```text
maximum of:
- bare-earth DTM
- DTM + vegetation height
- building surface height
```

For long lines of sight, include Earth curvature and optionally atmospheric refraction.

## Objective metrics I would calculate

Rather than immediately producing a single opaque "beauty score", calculate several transparent dimensions.

### 1. Visible ground area

Calculate visible terrain within several radii:

```text
visible_area_2km
visible_area_5km
visible_area_20km
visible_area_50km
```

The multiple radii matter. A large nearby valley feels different from a distant flat horizon, even when their total visible map areas are similar.

### 2. Open azimuth

Measure the proportion of the 360-degree panorama that is relatively unobstructed.

For example:

```text
open_azimuth = proportion of directions
where no nearby tree, hill or building rises above a chosen visual angle
```

Also record the largest continuous open sector. A 150-degree continuous panorama is more informative than many tiny gaps adding up to 150 degrees.

### 3. Directional relative relief

Absolute altitude is less important than altitude relative to what is in front of the observer.

For each direction and distance ring, calculate something similar to:

```text
directional_drop = observer elevation
                   - median terrain elevation in that sector
```

Use rings such as:

* 0.5-2km
* 2-5km
* 5-20km

This captures exactly the Coombe Hill situation: a point does not have to be a very high mountain if there is a broad drop on one side.

### 4. Visible-distance distribution

For every azimuth, record the distance to the final visible point or horizon. Then calculate:

* median visible distance
* 90th-percentile visible distance
* longest continuous sectors with distant visibility

This is more robust than using only maximum distance, which can be produced by one narrow gap between trees.

### 5. Near-field obstruction

Trees 30m away matter much more than trees 5km away. Measure the fraction of the visual field blocked within:

* 50m
* 250m
* 1km

I would strongly penalise viewpoints where vegetation occupies a large portion of the near-field horizon.

### 6. Angular visible area

Ordinary viewshed area counts every square metre equally. But a distant square kilometre occupies much less of your visual field than a nearby square kilometre.

Add a second metric that approximately weights visible cells by their angular size. Distance weighting, terrain slope and orientation can all be incorporated. This should correspond more closely to what fills the observer's panorama.

### 7. Visible landscape composition

Intersect the viewshed with Living England habitats and calculate:

* percentage woodland
* percentage grassland or heath
* percentage arable land
* percentage water
* percentage urban land
* number and diversity of habitat classes
* distribution of these classes by distance

This remains objective. You are not asserting that water or woodland is beautiful, only describing what is visible.

### 8. Accessibility

Keep accessibility separate from visual quality:

* distance from a path
* walking distance from parking or public transport
* ascent required
* whether the point is on access land
* path surface and steepness, where available

Then the website can offer filters such as "best view within a 20-minute walk" without confusing scenic quality with convenience.

## Avoid arbitrary beauty weights initially

A formula such as:

```text
score =
  visible area
+ openness
+ relative relief
+ landscape diversity
- nearby obstruction
- urban coverage
```

looks objective, but its weights are still subjective.

A better first version is to:

1. Convert each metric into its percentile among English candidate points.
2. Display a radar chart or component scores.
3. Use Pareto ranking to find places that are not clearly dominated by another location.
4. Allow users to choose "wide panorama", "dramatic drop", "long-distance view" or "least urban".

You can later add a default composite score, but the raw dimensions should remain visible.

## Data-volume implications

England covers roughly 130,000 square kilometres. At 1m resolution, that is about 130 billion raster cells. A single uncompressed float32 national raster would be approximately 520GB, so a DTM and DSM together would be around 1TB before indexes, intermediate products and backups. ([GOV.UK][12])

Therefore:

* use the single 10m DTM for the countrywide calculation
* store the 1m data as tiled Cloud Optimised GeoTIFFs
* fetch or process only local 1m windows around promising candidates
* cache horizon profiles and final scores rather than repeatedly running viewsheds

The Environment Agency download service distributes raster data in 5km GeoTIFF tiles and point clouds in LAZ format. It also supports requests for larger areas, although a complete national high-resolution request is operationally much heavier than downloading selected tiles. ([Defra Data Services][13])

## Important limitations

**LiDAR age varies by location.** A national composite combines surveys from different years. Read the accompanying metadata index and show the survey date on your site where possible. ([Data.gov.uk][1])

**Many surveys were conducted in winter.** Deciduous woodland may have been scanned with fewer leaves, so raw DSM visibility can be too optimistic for summer. Treat mapped tree canopy as substantially opaque even when the LiDAR return contains gaps. ([Defra Data Services][14])

**The vegetation model excludes shorter vegetation.** The VOM primarily represents vegetation taller than 2.5m, while an English hedge around 2m can still block a human observer. Use first-return DSM minus DTM for near-field hedge detection rather than relying only on the VOM. ([Data.gov.uk][4])

**First-return DSM contains noise.** Cars, temporary structures and classification errors can appear as obstructions. Remove small isolated objects and compare the DSM against the VOM and building polygons. ([Defra Data Services][14])

**Path data is not equivalent to legal access.** OSM is excellent for discovery and routing, but the local authority's Definitive Map and Statement remains the authoritative source for public rights of way. Your interface should distinguish "mapped path" from "confirmed public right of way". ([Data.gov.uk][15])

## Recommended MVP stack

For the first version, I would use:

```text
Nationwide candidate discovery:
- EA LIDAR Composite DTM 10m
- OpenStreetMap England extract
- Natural England CRoW Access Layer

Detailed scoring of shortlisted points:
- EA LIDAR Composite DTM 1m
- EA First Return DSM 1m
- EA Vegetation Object Model
- Living England Habitat Map

Website:
- candidate and access data in PostGIS
- raster tiles in object storage as Cloud Optimised GeoTIFFs
- MapLibre for the interactive map
```

A good first pilot would be the Chilterns rather than all England. Run the algorithm without telling it where Coombe Hill is, then check whether Coombe Hill Monument appears near the top for open azimuth, one-sided relative relief and low near-field obstruction. That gives you a useful validation case without using human ratings as training data.
