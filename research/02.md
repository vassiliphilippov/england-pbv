Yes. Your idea corresponds closely to an established family of GIS measurements called **viewshed analysis**, and there is now at least one public project that has done almost exactly what you describe on a global scale.

A normal viewshed answers:

> From this location, which parts of the surrounding terrain are visible?

A **total viewshed**, also called an **outgoing visibility index**, repeats that calculation for every possible observation point and records how much territory each point can see. High values tend to occur on exposed summits, ridges, escarpment edges, isolated hills and places overlooking substantially lower terrain. ([GRASS][1])

## The closest existing project: All The Views

**All The Views** is almost a direct implementation of your theory. It evaluates locations across the Earth's land surface and produces a heat map based on:

* total visible surface area from each location
* longest unobstructed line of sight
* terrain elevation
* Earth curvature
* atmospheric refraction

The project's global calculation used an elevation grid at approximately 100 metre spacing and tested lines of sight in 360 directions from each point. Its interactive map lets you identify places from which exceptionally large areas of terrain should be visible. ([Tom Bh][2])

This is probably the closest public answer to your question.

Its main limitation is resolution. A roughly 100 metre global elevation model is good at finding mountain ridges, escarpments and isolated hills, but it cannot reliably tell whether the exact observation point is behind a line of trees, a hedge, a building or a small local rise. So it should identify **geographically promising zones**, rather than the exact best place to stand.

## Other existing tools

### QGIS Visibility Analysis

The open-source **Visibility Analysis** plugin for QGIS can calculate ordinary viewsheds, cumulative viewsheds and total viewsheds over a selected region. Unlike a global web map, it lets you choose the elevation data, observer height, maximum viewing distance and the candidate locations to analyse. ([QGIS Plugins][3])

This is probably the best existing tool for building a high-resolution version for England.

### GRASS GIS

GRASS GIS has several relevant modules:

* `r.viewshed` calculates everything visible from one point.
* `r.viewshed.cva` combines visibility from multiple observation points.
* `r.viewshed.exposure` can score visibility using distance, slope, aspect, visual magnitude and solid angle, rather than simply counting square kilometres.

The last module is particularly relevant because an area occupying a large part of your field of vision should normally contribute more than a distant strip of land that is technically visible but almost imperceptible. ([GRASS][4])

### HeyWhatsThat and UpToWhere

**HeyWhatsThat** and **UpToWhere** are online tools that calculate the visible terrain and horizon from a selected location. They are useful for investigating a place you have already found, but they generally do not exhaustively search a country and rank every possible viewpoint. ([heywhatsthat.com][5])

### SAGA Topographic Openness

SAGA GIS calculates **topographic openness**, which measures how enclosed or exposed a point is by examining surrounding horizon angles. A summit surrounded by low horizon angles has high positive openness; a point in a valley has low openness. This is closely related to your "clearness of the horizon" idea. ([SAGA GIS][6])

## Your theory is largely right, but visible area alone is insufficient

Visible area is an excellent first metric, but it can produce some strange winners.

For example:

* A low cliff overlooking a completely flat sea may have an enormous theoretical viewable area.
* A point overlooking a flat agricultural plain might beat a dramatic mountain valley in square kilometres.
* A very distant, narrow strip of visible terrain can add considerable map area while occupying almost no space in the actual human visual field.
* A point may have a huge view in one direction but be enclosed by forest in every other direction.
* An industrial landscape, quarry or airport may score highly even though many people would not call it beautiful.

These are not failures of geometry. They show that **expansiveness** is objective, while **beauty** requires some definition of what visual characteristics matter.

A stronger objective system would measure several independent properties.

### 1. Visible ground area at several distances

Instead of one total, calculate visible area within:

* 0-2 km
* 2-10 km
* 10-40 km
* beyond 40 km

A strong viewpoint generally has visual depth: foreground, middle distance and distant horizon. A place where everything visible lies 30 kilometres away can feel less impressive than one with multiple layers.

### 2. Visible solid angle

Measure how much of the observer's actual visual field is occupied by visible terrain.

Solid angle gives more importance to:

* large nearby slopes
* deep valleys immediately below the observer
* mountains occupying a large vertical angle
* substantial foreground and middle-distance features

It gives less importance to tiny distant areas that add many square kilometres to the map but only a thin line to the panorama. GRASS's exposure calculations and research on the **Vertical Visibility Index** address this weakness of standard two-dimensional viewsheds. ([GRASS][7])

### 3. Horizontal openness

Measure:

* what percentage of the full 360 degrees is unobstructed
* the longest continuous open sector
* average horizon elevation angle
* whether there are one, two or several open directions

This distinguishes a narrow window through trees from an uninterrupted panoramic viewpoint.

### 4. Relative elevation and downward view

Absolute altitude is less important than altitude relative to the surrounding terrain.

A 250 metre escarpment overlooking land at 80 metres can provide a much more expansive view than a 700 metre point surrounded by 680 metre hills. Useful measurements include:

* elevation above the median surrounding terrain
* steepness of the initial drop
* amount of terrain lying below the observer
* topographic prominence
* distance to higher obstructing ground

This is exactly why escarpment edges often make outstanding viewpoints.

### 5. Terrain depth and relief

A completely flat visible plain may be expansive but visually simple. You can objectively measure:

* variation in visible elevations
* number of distinguishable ridges
* depth between foreground and background
* visible slope diversity
* skyline complexity

These metrics do not require beauty ratings, although deciding whether more complexity is always better would still be a design choice.

### 6. Vegetation and building obstruction

This requires distinguishing between two elevation models:

* **DTM**, digital terrain model: bare ground with trees and buildings removed
* **DSM**, digital surface model: includes tree canopies, buildings and other surface objects

You could calculate the same viewshed twice:

* DTM viewshed = geographical potential
* DSM viewshed = likely currently visible area
* difference = obstruction caused by vegetation and structures

England is unusually suitable for this. The Environment Agency provides approximately 1 metre LiDAR-based DTM and DSM coverage for about 99 percent of England. The DSM represents surface objects such as trees and buildings, while the DTM attempts to represent the underlying ground. ([Defra Data Services][8])

That is sufficient resolution to distinguish many woodland edges, tree belts, buildings and small local landforms that the global All The Views map cannot resolve.

## A genuinely objective ranking method

To avoid inserting arbitrary human preferences, I would not initially combine everything into one "beauty score". Instead, represent every candidate point by a vector:

[
V(p) =
(A_{2}, A_{10}, A_{40}, \Omega, H, D, R, -O)
]

Where:

* (A_2, A_{10}, A_{40}) are visible areas within different radii
* (\Omega) is visible solid angle
* (H) is horizontal openness
* (D) is depth across distance bands
* (R) is relative relief above surrounding terrain
* (O) is obstruction from trees and buildings

Then identify **Pareto-optimal viewpoints**: locations for which no other location is better on every metric.

That would be more objectively defensible than assigning subjective weights. A user could then filter the results:

* largest panoramas
* steepest downward views
* best unobstructed horizons
* strongest mixture of near and distant scenery
* best viewpoints without a long climb

Accessibility can be added later without changing the visibility calculation.

## Would this method find Coombe Hill?

Very probably.

Coombe Hill Monument is around 260 metres above sea level and overlooks the much lower Aylesbury Vale and Oxford Plain. The National Trust describes extensive views from the monument, open steep slopes and broad visibility across the vale. ([National Trust][9])

Its important features are not merely its absolute height:

* it lies near an escarpment edge
* the ground drops substantially on the vale side
* much of the foreground is open grassland
* the horizon is comparatively unobstructed
* the visible landscape extends through several depth layers
* the observer stands above a large amount of lower terrain

Your photograph shows this structure well. There is an open foreground, a relatively abrupt drop, a large middle-distance plain and a distant horizon. Inference: Coombe Hill should score particularly highly on relative elevation, directional openness, distance depth and DSM-adjusted visible area, even if it is not the highest ground in the wider region.

## Existing research goes beyond simple viewsheds

There are academic methods that calculate viewshed size for every cell in a landscape, automatically choose observation points that maximise terrain coverage, and use optimisation algorithms to find a small set of exceptionally effective viewpoints. ([Taylor & Francis Online][10])

More recent work has also used airborne LiDAR and machine learning to produce detailed visibility maps that include terrain and vegetation structure. Such studies confirm that vegetation characteristics can be major predictors of actual visibility, meaning a bare-earth elevation model alone is often inadequate. ([USFS Research & Development][11])

## Practical conclusion

There are three levels of solution:

1. **All The Views** is the closest ready-made global project. It can reveal broad geographical hotspots immediately.

2. **QGIS or GRASS with ordinary elevation data** can search a county or region more systematically and at better resolution.

3. **QGIS or custom Python with England's 1 metre DTM and DSM LiDAR** could identify exact clear viewpoints, account for woodland and buildings, compare several viewing radii and rank local maxima.

For discovering places comparable to Coombe Hill within driving distance of Chorleywood, the third approach would be considerably more accurate than searching only for high elevations. The key calculation should be total outgoing viewshed plus solid angle, relative relief, horizon openness and the difference between DTM and DSM visibility.
